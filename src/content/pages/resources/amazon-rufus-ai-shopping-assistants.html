What's happening now

At Freak we have started to see material changes to ecommerce numbers, including revenue, as large language models such as ChatGPT and Amazon's own assistant Rufus begin to replace search and paid clicks. They have already taken a real slice of the two biggest digital channels most brands have, across Amazon, Google and brand sites.

The way shoppers find products is changing. Rufus moves beyond keyword search. A shopper asks a question in plain language, and instead of pages of listings the assistant offers a handful of highly relevant products. Amazon has done this before with Alexa, and both work in natural, conversational language.

Many brands still aren't aware of Rufus, or haven't connected it to what they're seeing in their numbers. That's why we wrote this.

The good news

Our data shows that brands with a strong foundation are better placed to be favoured by assistants like Rufus. That means complete product information, a body of positive reviews and clear brand messaging, because the assistant learns from exactly that data.

It makes sense. A language model takes a huge amount of information and refines it down to an answer for one question. Bigger brands simply contribute more data for the model to learn from, and that gives them an advantage. These models are designed not to give the same answer every time, but in our user journey testing on Amazon, the range of products bought through Rufus has narrowed sharply.

The bad news

AI curation means fewer products make the shortlist, and being one of the few the assistant picks is becoming critical. A brand that ranks first for a key search term can now be fifth in the assistant's answer, because of the randomness built into these models.

For bigger brands there is another consequence. Some of our clients hold 80% of the top ten listings for key search terms. In an assistant's answer they might get one of five places, which is 20% of that digital storefront. And where they used to hold the top spot, they can now be anywhere from first to fifth.

Let's be clear. If every Amazon sale went through Rufus today, it would be a volcanic reshaping of the market.

What brands should do now

The rise of assistants like Rufus calls for a shift in how brands approach Amazon. These are the five things we focus on with our clients.

1. Write for conversation, not just keywords

Product pages need to speak the language of conversational AI. That means answering the questions shoppers actually ask, in the bullets and the A+ content, and weaving the brand story and unique selling points in a way the assistant can understand. If your yoga mat is made from recycled rubber, say so in words that match a question like "eco friendly yoga mats".

2. Treat reviews and Q&A as inputs

Positive reviews and detailed questions and answers feed straight into the AI. Encourage genuine, detailed reviews and make sure reviews that break Amazon's guidelines are reported, because the assistant will use that data to judge whether a product suits and can be trusted.

3. Give the AI complete data

AI thrives on complete, accurate information. Fill in every backend attribute and use A+ content for both the brand story and the detailed specifications an assistant needs to recommend you with confidence.

4. Keep the foundations strong

Organic rank and a positive sales history still matter. They build the authority that makes a product more likely to be recognised and recommended, and well planned advertising helps hold that visibility while assistants mature.

5. Keep testing and adapting

Rufus is early and will keep changing. We monitor how it behaves in each category, test content and strategy and adapt quickly so our clients stay visible and favoured.

In short
  • AI assistants answer with a few products, not pages of results
  • Strong, complete, well reviewed listings are favoured
  • Brands with many top positions can lose share of the storefront
  • Write for questions, complete every attribute and look after reviews

The brands that will win

AI shopping assistants are transforming ecommerce. They promise shoppers a more personal, efficient way to buy, and they ask more of brands. Those that adapt their content and strategy to how these systems find and rank products will be the ones that are not just discoverable but recommended.
